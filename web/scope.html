Everything a converter does takes time: what you hear of the track left this page a moment ago, and what the microphone heard of your answer arrives a moment late. Alignment delays the track to meet the input — the only direction that works, since the input cannot be made earlier. Start from the suggestion and trim by eye until a note you play lands on the beat it belongs to.

Your playing is drawn and never monitored: the track is already going into the same microphone, and sending that back out is a feedback loop. The two clocks also drift — the converter counts samples at its own rate and the page counts at its own — so over a long session the alignment walks and wants nudging again. That is a known limit rather than something handled.

A rack is whatever you tick plus whatever files you add, and the two boxes are the parts that are not files. Files go in beside the ones already there; the × on a lane takes it out again. The generator and the live input together, with no track between them, is what you want for playing along with something the page is making — and X–Y can pair either against either.

Each is one lane, which for the generator means its left channel: a lane is one signal and the generator makes two, so its own figure is not available while it is a lane, and a note sets its pitch without starting or stopping it. Hear the generator sends that left channel to the rack's mix. The input is never heard, which is the microphone rule unchanged.

The count beside Add files is how many lanes your choice comes to, out of six; the two boxes take their places before any file does.

Add files, or drop them on the page, and each becomes a lane. They share one clock and one timebase — lanes on different timebases cannot be compared, which is the only reason to stack them. Solo and mute are the monitor mix; show is the screen. Nothing is uploaded.

Audio
What is it

A microphone is never sent to the speakers: what comes out of them goes back into it, and that is a feedback loop. A line input has no ear — a keyboard does not hear the room — so it may be monitored, and that is a claim you are making about what is plugged in rather than something the browser can tell. Get it wrong with a microphone selected and the loop is real, so wear headphones the first time.

What you hear that way has been round the converter twice and through the page's own buffers: the figure given is the browser's own, which is the output side only and a floor rather than the whole trip. Tens of milliseconds is normal and a keyboard player will feel it. The setup that does not suffer it is hearing the instrument directly and letting this page only look. A band split is not monitored at all.

Turns the figure on the screen. Worth little by hand and a great deal pointed at something: send Level to it and the picture turns as you play, which is the difference between a shape that sits there and one that answers.

On a stereo pair it turns the signal: the lanes are rotated before the trigger sees them, so Y–T, the figure and — on the shaped side — the speakers are all the same rotation. On a rack of stems, a band split or the lag lane it only turns the figure, because those are not a stereo image and mixing two stems together is not what the knob says it does.

Numbers read is what the cells along the bottom, the tuner and the correlation bar are about. Signal is what arrived and is the default, so turning the picture leaves every reading exactly where it was — the same arithmetic on the same samples, not a close copy. Turned points them at what is on the screen, and the readout says post-rotation so a number is never quietly about something other than its label. The level meter and the Clipping verdict stay on the signal either way: one is a modulation source and would feed back into the picture, and the other is a claim about the input.

It runs on the samples rather than in the audio graph, so it reaches the test tone as well — which is arithmetic and has no audio graph at all. Drop Full scale under Vertical, or the amplitude.

The two switches are separate because they answer different questions, and they straddle the same block: the filter, the AC coupling and the X–Y rotation, in that order. Screen shaped, speakers input is the block as an instrument for looking. Both shaped is the ordinary sense. Screen input, speakers shaped lets you watch what you are feeding it, and the readout says screen on the input so a flat trace under a filtered sound is never a mystery — and speakers on the input the other way round, because a sweep on the graticule over a plain tone is the same confusion mirrored. Neither is said when there is nothing switched on for them to differ about. Anything that reaches the speakers passes a limiter first, which is not a taste decision: resonance is something a source can sweep, and a resonant peak swept upward is a loud surprise in a pair of headphones.

The screen’s switch used to straddle the filter alone while the speakers’ straddled all three, so Input meant one thing to the eyes and another to the ears — on a page whose argument is that they are the same block. The lag is the one thing still outside it, because the speakers have no lag to bypass yet.

Shaped is the filter and the X–Y rotation, and the rotation is the interesting one: turning a figure and rotating a stereo image are the same 2×2 matrix, so the control that exists to make the picture prettier is a real stereo operation and this is where you hear it. Both are driven from one set of numbers — the screen and the speakers cannot disagree about what a rotation is, and a test renders the graph and checks it against the arithmetic.

Rotation is seen whether or not it is heard, and on a stereo pair it reaches the Y–T lanes and the trigger as well as the figure — it is a transform on the signal, so it is in the block before anything looks at it. What it does not reach is the numbers: those read Signal by default, under Display, and stay about what arrived. The level meter and the Clipping verdict stay there whatever that switch says. Turning the stereo image can put up to 41% more into one channel than either started with, which the limiter and the clamp are still the last things before.

Oscilloscope
How this works
  • An oscilloscope draws what a signal is doing. Three ways of drawing it, and the switch marked Display chooses.
  • Y–T is the waveform Amplitude up, time across. Timebase sets how much time is on screen, in the 1–2–5 steps a scope has always stepped in.
  • X–Y plots one channel against the other Time is thrown away. Two tones of the same frequency draw a line, an ellipse or a circle depending only on the angle between them; simple ratios close into figures.
  • One input has no second axis — so lag makes one A single instrument down a single input gives two identical channels, and identical channels draw a diagonal line and nothing else, whatever the preset. Lag X–Y, on the Bench under Picture → Vertical, plots the signal against itself a moment earlier instead. A held note becomes a closed loop whose shape is its harmonic content, and it moves when you move.

    This is a phase portrait, not a Lissajous figure: the second axis is the same signal, so what you are reading is how the sound changes over that moment rather than how two sounds relate. Auto sets the delay to a quarter of a period, which is what makes a pure tone a circle, and it locks slowly on purpose — chasing the pitch would hold the circle round through vibrato and hide the very thing worth seeing. Locked slowly, vibrato makes the loop turn.

    Heard is how much of the delayed copy reaches the right speaker, when the speakers are on the shaped side. Heard, a signal plus itself a moment later is a comb filter that tracks the pitch. In headphones it cuts deepest at half, and in a room at full. It starts at 20%, which is mostly width, and stops at 50% — the top of the slider is the setting where, in headphones, the right ear loses some of a note's harmonics entirely. That is allowed on purpose; it is where the effect is strongest.
  • AC coupling, and where its corner sits Under Vertical, per lane. It blocks what is not moving, so a converter with a DC offset does not push the trace off the screen — a one-pole high pass, the same one a bench scope puts in front of its input. The corner is where it starts taking things away: at ten hertz, which is where a scope usually sits, a slow drift goes and the signal does not; down at one hertz almost nothing is removed quickly, which is what you want when the drift IS the signal; up at twenty it thins the bottom of a bass note as well.

    The corner is worth knowing about because the numbers under the trace are about the coupled signal, not the raw one. At ten hertz that moves a peak reading by a per cent or two, and rather more on anything near the corner; at one hertz an asymmetric signal — a half-wave rectified tone, say — can read a third higher, because a pole that slow has not finished taking the offset out within the window. That is what AC coupling at one hertz means rather than a fault, and the readout names the corner while it is on so the numbers say which signal they are about.

    The same corner reaches the speakers when they are on the shaped side, from one set of numbers: the screen and the speakers cannot disagree about what AC coupling is.
  • Spect is a spectrogram Frequency up, time running right to left, level as ink. Hover it and it names the frequency and the note under the pointer.
  • Zoom magnifies, and the wheel works In Y–T it shows a slice of the capture stretched, centred on the trigger — the record is unchanged, so the measurements still read all of it. In X–Y it scales the figure; on the spectrogram it closes in the frequency band.
  • A filter, on two taps Under Filter. It reshapes the trace, which is the point of having one here: a low pass rounds a square's corners toward a sine, a notch carves a harmonic out of the picture, a resonant sweep draws its own ringing. Screen reads and Speakers get are separate switches, so the filter can be an instrument for looking while what you hear is untouched, or both, or the other way round. Resonance is nought for gentle and a hundred for extreme, and at nought a low pass is maximally flat. It runs on the samples rather than in the audio graph, so it reaches the test tone too — which is arithmetic and has no audio graph at all. Point a source at the cutoff and it sweeps.
  • Persistence is the phosphor How slowly the screen forgets. Off for a waveform; long or infinite for X–Y, where the figure is built up a pass at a time.
  • The photocell: the picture moving the controls Photocell, above the screen, puts a light sensor on it — drag the reticle onto the picture, or give it focus and move it a cell at a time with the arrow keys. How bright the phosphor is under it becomes a source, Photocell, which can be put on a control like an LFO: park it on the edge of a figure and it pulses as the beam sweeps past, drop it inside a solid and it reads how dense it is. Point it at rotation or zoom and the figure moves itself — and moves what the photocell sees, which is the loop. It fills with its own reading, so you can see what it is seeing.

    It can reach any control, the filter and the generator included, so the picture can be heard as well as seen. Three things keep that loop from running away. It moves a control at most half as far as another source could, and every source reading the picture on one control shares that half between them — three of them on the cutoff move it no further than one. Its reading can change no faster than two full swings a second, so it cannot flip back and forth every frame. And the limiter is still last, so a loop that finds a resonance is caught rather than heard at full scale. Persistence is its memory: long persistence makes it slow and smooth, short makes it twitchy. Over a spectrogram it reads nothing, because no beam draws one. Clear, beside Panes, wipes the screen and what the photocell sees, and leaves persistence as it was set.
  • What the picture says, and what the loop is doing With the photocell on, five more sources read the whole picture rather than one point, under the same rules. Roundness is one for a circle and nought for a line — and one for a square too, because it sees how spread a figure is, not its corners. Coverage is how much of the screen is lit. Change is how much the picture moved since the last frame. Novelty is how far the picture is from anything it showed in the last fifteen seconds: nought when it keeps coming back, high when it has gone somewhere new. Direction is which way round the beam travels, plus or minus. Boredom climbs slowly while the picture neither moves nor goes anywhere new, and drains once it does: put it on something that shakes the figure and a loop that has settled will, after a few seconds, nudge itself out again. It reaches less than the others and can never climb faster than a quarter a second.

    The readout says what the loop is doing: settled when nothing moves, cycling when it keeps coming back to where it has been, wandering when it moves and does not repeat, running away when the screen fills or the limiter is working hard to hold the sound down. The interesting setups are the wandering ones: two sources crossed onto two controls — say Roundness on the lag, with lag X–Y on, and Novelty on rotation — and persistence long enough to give it memory.
  • The beam has a brightness On a cathode-ray tube the spot's brightness is how long it dwelt on a spot, which means it goes as one over its speed. The trace turns bright where it slows to turn around and faint through the fast sweeps — which is most of why a real scope does not look like a plot. A circle comes out perfectly even, because a circle traced at a steady rate really does have constant speed.
  • Where it applies Settings → Beam. On for X–Y, and off for the waveform by default: dimming the steep edge of a square wave fights the measuring that Y–T is for. Off / subtle / moderate / high widens both the range of tone and how much speed it takes to cross it. On a very long timebase the waveform falls back to a flat trace, because past two samples a pixel the screen is showing each column's range rather than a path, and a range has no speed.
  • Panes What sits down the right-hand side. Turn them all off and the trace takes the whole width.
  • Scope, the Bench and Settings Scope is for watching and playing: the screen has the window, and the strip along the top keeps the trigger, the timebase, the zoom and persistence. The Bench is where everything is tuned: the controls take the middle of the window and the screen sits beside them, still running, so you can watch a figure answer with your hand on the control. On a wide window the Bench stops at the width three columns need rather than spreading them apart. Settings opens from either and holds what you set once and leave — MIDI, Audio, Presets and Beam. Every section lives in one of the two places and nowhere else.

    The Bench is five tabs, by task: Play (the source, the keyboard, the generator), Picture (the lanes, the display, the vertical scale, the trigger), Shape (the filter), Sources and Measure. Each section's title carries ⋯, and behind it is that section's explanation and the handful of settings reached for rarely. The source chips stay beside the screen on every tab, so a chip can be dragged onto a control wherever it is.

    Press / to find any control by name — it knows a few other words for things, so adsr finds the envelope and lfo the oscillators — and choosing a result takes you to it, opening whatever it is behind. B switches between Scope and the Bench, and the view and the tab are remembered.
  • The Sources tab Every source, by family: the oscillators, the signal's level and envelope, your hands on the keyboard, the controllers you have moved, and the picture itself. A family with nothing in it says how to get something. Choose one and it says what it is, shows where it is right now, and lists every control it is on with its own depth and a cross to take it off; Add a destination puts it on another. An oscillator's shape and rate are there, and a controller can be renamed or forgotten there. Choosing a source also makes every control it is on, on every tab, draw its sweep.
  • The trigger is what makes the trace stand still. Without it every frame starts at a different point of the wave and the picture scrolls.
  • Level and edge The scope waits for the signal to cross this value going that way, and starts drawing there. The dashed rose line is the level.
  • Position puts the window before the edge At 10% you see a tenth of the screen's worth of what happened before the trigger. The marker at the top is where t = 0.
  • Holdoff rejects an edge with another close behind it Which settles a waveform that crosses the level more than once a cycle. Stated as a quiet interval before an edge rather than a lockout after one, because this redraws a sliding buffer rather than sweeping once per trigger.
  • Auto, Normal, Single Auto draws whatever arrived, triggered or not. Normal holds the last triggered frame rather than showing an untriggered one. Single arms, catches one edge and stops — which is how you catch a transient.
  • Free run is not a fault When nothing crosses the level the trace goes grey and keeps moving. A signal with no edge in it is silence, not an error. The only thing here painted in rust is clipping.
  • Autoset Reads the signal and picks a timebase, a level and a vertical scale that fit it.
  • Everything the scope can tell you about the signal is along the bottom, one row per channel.
  • The numbers Peak and RMS in dBFS, peak-to-peak, frequency, period, the nearest note, duty cycle, and total harmonic distortion. fs is the level that reaches the top of the graticule.
  • They are about the signal, not about the picture Full scale and zoom are where the trace sits and how big it is, and neither touches a number here. X–Y rotation is the one shared transform that does reach the samples — on a stereo pair it turns the lanes themselves, and the speakers with them — so these numbers read the signal as it arrived unless you say otherwise. Numbers read → Turned, on the Bench under Picture → Display, points them at what is on the screen instead, and the readout says post-rotation so you can tell. The level meter and the Clipping verdict stay on the signal either way.
  • Cursors are draggable Turn them on under the Bench's Measure tab. Two in time and two in amplitude; the readout gives the difference between each pair, and Δt as a frequency.
  • The longest timebase depends on the converter The scope reads its history out of an analyser, and Web Audio caps one at 32768 samples — 743 ms at 44.1 kHz, but only 341 at 96, because that cap is a count of samples rather than a length of time. So the slowest sweeps are available on one machine and not on another, and the timebase says past the buffer rather than leaving you to notice a trace that stops early. The readout gives the shortfall in milliseconds when it happens.
  • THD only answers when there is a tone It needs a real spectral line — a local maximum standing well above the band. Noise is all peaks, so it says nothing rather than inventing a number.
  • The harmonics pane The fundamental in ink and its partials beside it. A square wave shows odd harmonics tall and even ones almost absent, and about 41% distortion — the first eight partials' worth, which is what this counts. The generator's own waveforms are band-limited, so what you are reading is the waveform rather than the arithmetic that drew it.
  • A reference trace Measure → Save reference, on the Bench, freezes what is on screen and keeps it dashed behind the live trace, so you can see what changed.
  • The goniometer Left against right, turned 45°. Upright is mono, a circle is wide, and horizontal means the two channels are fighting each other.
  • Four sources, and a generator with four minds of its own. Play → Input, on the Bench.
  • Tone, Mic, File — or Lanes The tone is arithmetic, and runs the moment the page opens. Mic asks the browser for your input. File takes a WAV or MP3 — or drop one anywhere on the page — and plays it as it draws. Each of those is one signal, in stereo, so X–Y draws its left against its right.

You build it: tick the generator or the live input, add files, and take any lane out again with the × beside it. Choosing Lanes with nothing in it changes nothing on the screen until you add something, and coming back to it after the tone brings back what you had. Drop several files on the page and they become a rack of their own, or join the one that is loaded.
  • Playing along with a track Lanes → Play along opens a backing track and your microphone as two lanes of one rack — so X–Y is the track against you, which is the figure worth having. Your playing is drawn and never sent to the speakers: the track is already going into the same microphone and sending that back out is a feedback loop.

    Alignment is the control that matters.   • Playing live: split the input into bands Under Mic, Split → Into bands puts what you are playing through the same four crossovers the stem splitter uses. The browser's echo cancellation, noise suppression and automatic gain are all turned off: they are built for voice calls and every one of them reshapes the waveform you came here to look at.
  • Microphone or line, and what may be heard A microphone is never sent to the speakers, because what comes out of them goes straight back into it. A line input — a keyboard, an interface, a mixer's output — has no ear, so there is no loop to make, and it can be monitored through the page behind an explicit toggle. Which one is plugged in is something you assert and the browser cannot check, so wear headphones the first time you turn it on.

    The cost is latency: what you hear has been through the converter and the page's buffers, which is tens of milliseconds and enough for a keyboard player to feel. The reading beside the toggle is the browser's own figure for the output side, so treat it as a floor. Hearing the instrument directly and letting this page only look is the version with no delay in it at all.
  • Up to six lanes Six because that is where the paper stops being able to tell one trace from the next — it is also what the largest stem separator produces. They share one clock: every lane starts at one scheduled instant, so what you are comparing is the same moment of the track. Nothing is uploaded; the files are decoded here.
  • One timebase, one trigger Lanes on different timebases cannot be compared, which is the only reason to stack them, so there is one. One lane provides the edge and every lane is cut at that index — pick which under Trigger → Source. It is how a four-channel bench scope works, for the same reason.
  • Stacked or overlaid Stacked gives each lane its own band and its own name, and answers what is each part doing. Overlaid puts them on one graticule and answers how do they line up. Press Autoset with a rack loaded and each lane is scaled to its own level, which is what makes a pad under a kick visible at all.
  • X–Y across two lanes Not just left against right. Bass against kick says whether they are fighting for the same instant; two melodic parts draw the interval between them. The goniometer follows the same pair.
  • The generator and your playing, on one graticule Under Lanes, tick Add the generator as a lane and Add the live input as a lane. No backing track needed: that combination is the whole point — what the page is making and what you are playing, on one graticule, with X–Y able to pair either against either. Split into bands Four filters, and honest about it — a kick and a hi-hat land in different lanes because they are at different frequencies, not because anything worked out that one is a drum. It answers which part of the mix is that bump in a millisecond, with no model to download. Solo a band to hear the part you are looking at.
  • Hear the generator Off when the page opens, because an instrument for looking at signals should not make one until you ask. Switched on, the generator moves into the audio thread and out through the same chain a line input gets — so the filter, the AC coupling and the X–Y rotation shape what you hear as well as what you see, whenever Speakers get is on Shaped. The trace is then the waveform that went to the speakers, sample for sample, rather than a second run of the same arithmetic.
  • The envelope, and when it is in force Only while a keyboard is driving the waveform. With nothing plugged in the amplitude is the slider and nothing else, exactly as it always was. The drawn modes are never gated — a harmonograph is a continuous drawing and a note there sets a ratio rather than starting something, so plugging a keyboard in does not blank the screen between phrases. Where the gate is open with no key down, the readout says gated · no note held rather than leaving you to wonder.

    A second key over a held one changes the pitch without starting the envelope again, which is what makes a run of notes a phrase; glide slides between them. Envelope is also a source — send it to the rotation and the figure opens as the note does. It is not the same thing as Level, which measures whatever is being drawn rather than following a key.
  • Waveform Sine, square, triangle, saw, noise, a sine with a third harmonic, drawbars and morph. The interval sets the right channel against the left.

    Morph is one slider through sine, triangle, saw and square, crossfading between each pair, so the shape is something a source can move: put an oscillator, the pedal or the photocell on it and the timbre changes as you play. On a whole station it is exactly that shape. Its saw is the menu's half a cycle later, so the note does not thin out on the way from the triangle to the saw, as it would with the saw's fundamental upside down against the triangle's. Full depth on it is a station and a half either way.

    Drawbars are the organ's way of making a sound: nine sine partials at the footages of a tonewheel organ, each drawn from nought to eight on the Shape tab. 8′ is the note; 16′ is an octave under it and 5⅓′ a fifth above that, and the rest are the octaves, fifths and third above. Each step is about three decibels, as on the organ, and however many bars are out the wave stays inside the amplitude, so pulling more out changes the colour and not the level. With the drawbars chosen, the registration shows under the Shape menu — 00 8740 000 to start — and pressing it goes to the bars. Every bar is a destination: move a drawbar on your keyboard and put it on the same bar here. In X–Y at an interval the figure shows how the partials line up.

    A registration with the 16′ out, such as 88 8000 000, has a cycle twice the note's, and the readout's frequency is the sound's rather than the key's: an octave under, and it can wander between the two. The frequency readout counts crossings, and a registration with several bars at full can cross more often than once a cycle and read high. The tuner can read the key instead: with a keyboard, tick take the lag and the tuner from the note.
  • Intervals draw consonance A figure closes when the two frequencies are a ratio of small whole numbers, which is what a consonant interval is. In just tuning a fifth is exactly 2:3 and stands still; in equal it is slightly off, and the figure turns at the rate the interval beats.
  • Play it: a MIDI keyboard Settings → MIDI → Connect. Two held notes become the two channels, so the interval under your hands is the figure on the screen: hold a fifth and 2:3 stands still, walk through thirds and the figure walks with you. The lower note goes on X and the higher on Y whichever finger landed first, so the picture turns only when the interval does. A third note takes the place of the older of the two. Mono instead takes the newest note as the pitch and leaves the interval to the menu; Hold keeps the last interval above a single note, which is how you walk a figure up a melody with one finger.

    Poly sounds every note you hold, up to eight, each with its own envelope, on the waveform generator. A chord has more notes than the screen has axes, so Draw chooses which: the lowest drawn note goes on X and the others on Y, and by default the drawn ones are the bass and the melody — over comping, the line against the bass. Lowest, highest and most recent are the other choices, two to eight notes of them. Every note is heard in both ears, drawn or not, and the readout says how many are left out of the picture: drawing 2 of 5 notes. Each key sounds at its own pitch. Tune a chord to its lowest note, under the section's extra controls, makes a major triad exactly 4:5:6 so its figure closes — at the price that every note moves a few cents depending on what is under it, which is what just tuning a chord means. A chord is louder than a note; the limiter catches it. Four sources follow your hands and can be put on any control — Notes, Spread, Melody and Inner, which is how hard you played the notes that are not drawn.

    Tuning applies to what you play: just snaps the interval to its whole-number ratio and the figure stands still, equal takes the keys as they are and it turns at the rate the interval beats. A tempered fifth turns about three quarters of a time a second, which is worth seeing. A tempered third turns nearly nine times a second, which under persistence is a blur.

    A note is also the only pitch here that is not estimated, so the lag, the tuner and the timebase can be told to take it as read — no confidence gate, no second of settling. The tuner then reads the gap between the key you pressed and the pitch that came back, which on an organ is a real number rather than an error.

    A sustain pedal works as a pedal does: keys let go while it is down go on sounding until it lifts, and a key struck again under it and still held stays when it does. It is also a source, Sustain, with the keyboard's: half-way down reads a half, and it glides rather than steps, so a pedal on the level swells instead of clicking. The notes it is holding are held from the moment it passes half-way, as the MIDI specification has it for a switch. It is not learned as a controller beside that, so it shows once.

    Move a drawbar, a pedal or the swell and it becomes a source you can put on any control. Web MIDI needs a secure page and permission from whatever embeds it: the published artifact is an iframe that does not grant it, so this works on the GitHub Pages copy and not there, and Safari has no Web MIDI at all.
  • Two layers: a split, or one keyboard twice In Poly, Layers gives the waveform generator a second set of voices. Split sends every note from a key upwards to layer B and the rest to layer A — comping with the left hand, a melody with the right. Press at C4 beside it and play the key B should start from. Layered sends every note to both, so each is two voices at once.

    Edit B turns the generator's panel over to layer B, which has its own shape, level and envelope, its own drawbars and morph, and its own Draw rule, so the comping can draw three notes while the melody draws one. Its pitches are the keys it is given, and the kind, the modulation and the heard switch are the generator's — a sweep on the pitch bends both layers. B starts as a copy of A, so a split changes where the notes go and nothing else until you change it.

    By default each layer draws its own figure, overlaid: A in the accent and B in olive, and in Y–T as four lanes. A against B draws one figure instead: every note layer A draws on X and every note B draws on Y — the comping against the melody. With a split on, the readout says where, and when a layer is leaving notes out it says which: drawing A 2 of 4, B 1 of 1. A generator in a rack plays one layer: a lane is one signal, and B's picture would have nowhere to go.
  • No keyboard? Keys on the screen Show keys, in the Bench's Play tab, puts two octaves along the bottom of the page, and they play exactly what a MIDI keyboard would — the dyad, Hold, the envelope and the note-as-truth all work from them, and they work in the published artifact and in Safari, where MIDI does not. Drag across them for a glissando. A mouse is one finger, so to hold a dyad tick Latch: each tap then holds its note until tapped again. The computer keyboard plays too, laid out as music software does it — A is C, W C♯, S D and so on along two rows — with Z and X for octaves. While the keys are open those letters play notes rather than their shortcuts; close the keys to have the shortcuts back. Pedal, at the end of the keys, is a sustain pedal that stays down until pressed again, and holding Space while the keys are open is one you hold with your thumb. Closing the keys lifts it. With the keys open the waveform waits for a note, as it does with a keyboard plugged in.
  • Harmonograph Two pendulums an axis, running down. Wants X–Y and infinite persistence. A tiny detune is what turns a closed figure into a spirograph.
  • Figure A path written into the two channels, so X–Y traces a picture. Slow the trace rate right down and you watch the beam draw it.
  • Wireframe: three dimensions out of two channels A solid is turned, flattened onto the screen and written into the pair of channels — which is how oscilloscope music has always done 3D. Nothing carries the depth but the shape of the projection. Spin X/Y/Z are turns per second; set them to values that are not simple multiples of each other or the solid returns to the same attitude every few seconds and reads as a loop.
  • Why some edges get drawn twice The beam draws one unbroken line, and a cube is twelve separate edges meeting three at a corner — an odd number, which means no route uses every edge exactly once. So four of them are retraced. The alternative is jumping, and a jump is a permanent chord across the middle of the solid: at infinite persistence it never fades. A doubled line costs nothing, because drawing the same pixels twice looks identical. The octahedron meets four edges at every corner, so it needs no retracing at all.
  • Every control that can be moved says so At the right-hand end of a slider: a small hollow ring means nothing is on it, coloured dots mean something is. Press it and a panel opens for that one parameter — a row per source with an ordinary slider for the depth, a cross to take it off, and the sources not on it yet as buttons to add. That is the whole gesture, and it is the same with a mouse, a thumb or a keyboard.

    The sources are the two low-frequency oscillators, Level — how hard you are playing — what your hands are doing on a keyboard, every controller you have moved since it was connected, and with the photocell on, the picture itself. The Sources tab explains each one. Dragging a chip from beside the screen straight onto a control is the quick way when you already know where it goes.
  • The lane under a slider is the reading It only reads — there is nothing to aim at. The bar runs from the value you set by hand to where one source takes it, and the tick is where the whole patch has the control at this instant, so a page at rest still shows itself working.

    What the bar draws is the destination's own law, not a fraction of the slider. Full depth on frequency is an octave, so at 220 Hz the bar ends at 440 rather than at the end of a four-kilohertz track. Where a control has a range it cannot leave, a depth that would push past it is kept but the end of the bar dims to say it cannot get there.
  • Several sources on one control They add up, which is how every patch cable ever made behaves. The panel holds as many rows as there are, and the control itself looks and measures exactly the same with one on it or five — the dots do the counting and nothing stacks up underneath it. The lane draws one of them: whichever chip you last clicked in the side column, or else the first that is there. A depth set to nothing stays patched, because a patch parked at zero is a thing you meant.
  • Point Level at the picture Everything here points at anything, whatever the source is. The generator's parameters grey out when there is no generator — a microphone has no interval — but X–Y rotation, the lag delay, zoom and the filter are the picture's own and take a patch on any input. Level is how hard you are playing: send it to the rotation and the figure turns as you play, which is the difference between a shape that sits there and one that answers.
  • Presets are the front door — grouped, on the left of the strip. The Live group names nothing about the generator, so those land on whatever you are playing rather than replacing it.
  • Each one sets everything Including its timebase, because in X–Y the window decides how much of the figure is drawn at once. Anything a preset does not mention goes back to its default, so they cannot leave each other's settings behind.
  • Custom means you have moved off one Nothing is lost; the picker is just telling you the truth.
  • Saving and sharing Settings → Presets. Saved setups live in this browser and nowhere else; a setup code is the whole configuration as text, to copy and paste.
